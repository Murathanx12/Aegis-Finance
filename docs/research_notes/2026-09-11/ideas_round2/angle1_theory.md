## 0. Verdict first: LPPLS (Sornette) — the one Aegis already has in code

**Read this before spending another hour on `lppls`.** The task asked
specifically what the *non-Sornette* literature concluded about out-of-sample
value. The answer is materially negative, and it is negative in exactly the way
Aegis's own guardrails are built to detect.

The three load-bearing critiques, verified:

1. **Brée, Challet & Peirano (2013), *Quantitative Finance* 13(2), 275–280,
   "Prediction accuracy and sloppiness of log-periodic functions"** (arXiv
   1006.2010). LPPL functions are *sloppy* in the Sethna sense: the Hessian
   eigenvalues separate by orders of magnitude, and **t_c — the crash date, the
   only parameter anyone cares about — is a sloppy parameter**, i.e. squared
   residuals barely move when it moves. Their quantitative result on *synthetic
   data that is genuinely LPPL by construction* (AR(1) noise, calibrated to the
   1987 Hang Seng crash — the single best-fitting real case): the standard
   deviation on t_c is **about half of (t_c − t)**, so the 95% confidence window
   runs from *today* to *twice the horizon away*. Their words: "when a crash
   warning is issued, the crash can occur any day at 95% confidence." They
   further argue the 80% windows in the Sornette-group papers are
   "over-optimistic" — the published windows are *narrower* than this
   best-case synthetic benchmark, which is not possible if the uncertainty is
   honestly propagated.

2. **Brée & Joseph (2013), *International Review of Financial Analysis* 30,
   287–297, "Testing for financial crashes using the Log Periodic Power Law
   model."** Tested the standard LPPL across 11 Hang Seng crashes (1970–2008):
   only 7 conformed to the parameter constraints that Johansen & Sornette
   proposed **ex post**. They also document the false-positive/definition
   problem directly: Lin, Ren & Sornette (2009) claimed few false positives
   because all five identified S&P 500 bubbles "preceded well-known crashes" —
   but under a standard crash criterion (>15% drop within 12 weeks) there were
   **12 crashes in the period and only 2 of the 5 claimed matched one**.
   Conclusion: price alone is not sufficient to predict bursts.

3. **Grobys (2025), *International Review of Financial Analysis*,
   "Log-periodicity: Fact or fiction?"** — the newest and most damaging. The
   standard practice (calibrate LPPLS under bounds, then declare an "LPPLS
   signature" when an ADF/PP test on the *residuals* rejects a unit root at
   tabulated critical values) has **severe size distortion**: ADF tables for
   raw series do not apply to residuals from a constrained nonlinear fit. Using
   an i.i.d. return bootstrap that re-runs the *entire two-stage procedure* on
   each resample, the empirical rejection rate at a nominal 1% level is
   **84.8% (monthly) and 82.8% (daily)** on the S&P 500. Bootstrap critical
   values "overturn many purported signatures." This is a pure
   multiple-testing-and-wrong-null failure — the exact defect family Aegis
   catalogues.

Two further structural facts, both conceded inside the pro-LPPLS literature:
the fit **always** returns a t_c for any price series whether or not a bubble
exists (Nature HSSC 2025 review); and the DS-LPPLS confidence indicator needs
~12,500 repeated estimations plus **experience-based filtering conditions** to
separate good fits from bad — i.e. a human-tuned selection step sitting in the
middle of the "objective" indicator. Gustavsson, Levén & Sjögren (2016,
*Financial History Review* 23(2), 193–217; and the 2020 IJBASS follow-up) —
who are *sympathetic* to the model — find predicted end dates depend on **when
the prediction is made**, sometimes by many months, and recommend the observer
"follow a bubble as it develops" and wait for successive estimates to agree.
That recommendation is, operationally, a discretionary overlay, and an
after-the-fact one.

**Aegis verdict: `MECHANISM_REJECTED` for point forecasting of t_c;
`DEPRIORITIZED` for anything else.** LPPLS as currently used is a
sloppy-parameter estimator whose headline output is its least identified
parameter, validated against a null that is demonstrably the wrong null. No
idea below builds on it. If the `lppls` service stays in the codebase it should
carry a docstring pointing at Grobys (2025) and Brée et al. (2013) so the next
reader does not re-discover this — and its outputs should never be allowed to
size a position.

*(Honest caveat in the other direction: the literature is not unanimous that
the log-periodic structure is nothing. Demirer, Demos, Gupta & Sornette (2019,
Quantitative Finance 19(5), 843–858) report LPPLS multi-scale confidence
indicators have some predictive content for US bubble regimes — but that is a
Sornette-coauthored paper, which is exactly the provenance the task asked to
discount, and Grobys's size critique applies to its test statistic.)*

## 1. The counterparty's cost basis: disposition-effect overhang as an event CONDITIONER

*(Neuroscience/psychology of professional traders — the disposition effect as a tradeable counterparty error.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** When news arrives at a stock whose current institutional holders are sitting on a large unrealised capital *gain*, those holders' disposition-driven eagerness to realise the gain supplies extra selling into good news — so the price under-reacts and drifts, and the under-reaction is largest exactly when the sign of the news matches the sign of the holders' overhang.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** This is not a price signal wearing a new name, and that is the whole point. Grinblatt & Han's headline result is that **when capital-gains overhang is included as a regressor, the momentum effect disappears** — momentum is the noisy shadow of the overhang variable, not the reverse. Against Aegis's four killers: (a) *beta in disguise* — overhang is a holdings-derived cross-sectional variable with no mechanical market loading, and the tradeable object is an *interaction* (news sign × overhang sign) that is double-differenced, so a near-zero beta is a prediction rather than a hope; (b) *one era* — the mechanism is a mental-accounting primitive of the **holder base**, so its era-dependence should track the holder mix (retail vs index vs active), which is itself observable in the 13F panel and can go on the right-hand side instead of being the unexplained era; (c) *leakage* — the conditioner uses only 13F data public at a known filing lag, which Aegis can enforce exactly; (d) *tradability* — a genuine risk, handled in the control. Critically, **this is the conditional question Aegis's closed PEAD/reaction lanes never asked.** Those lanes tested "does the reaction drift?" pooled over all holders. Frazzini's result is that the unconditional drift is the average of a large effect and roughly nothing — a global negative on the pooled lane does not answer it (CLAUDE.md, scope-aware verdicts).

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** All three inputs exist already. (i) Build the reference price from the **13F institutional holdings panel** using Frazzini's construction: accumulate each manager's net purchases and the price at which they occurred into a holdings-weighted cost basis, then `overhang = (P − referencePrice)/P`. (ii) Take event dates and surprise sign from **IBES** (earnings surprise, recommendation changes) through 2024, and from the **~408k news headlines** for 2025–26. (iii) Take forward returns from **CRSP daily** through 2024 and **Alpaca daily bars** 2025–26. The test is one 5×5 double sort (surprise quintile × overhang quintile) plus a Fama-MacBeth with overhang, past return, turnover and size. Nothing new to build except the reference-price accumulator. Report **all five overhang quintiles within the top-surprise bucket** so the monotone shape is the claim, not one corner cell.

- **The control.** Three, and it must survive all of them. (1) **Sign-flip placebo** — the "negative overhang spread": long good-news stocks with the largest *losses*, short bad-news stocks with the largest *gains*. Frazzini reports this leg is **not significantly different from zero**; if Aegis finds both legs paying, the result is drift, not disposition, and it is momentum again. (2) **Shuffled-holder control** — keep each stock's event and its own price path but compute overhang from a randomly reassigned manager's holdings history, matched on manager size and turnover. If a random holder base reproduces the spread, the variable is proxying the price path, not the cost basis. (3) **Momentum-orthogonalisation**, per Grinblatt & Han: put 12-1 momentum and overhang in one regression and require overhang's t to survive while momentum's dies; the opposite outcome sends this to `FAILED_VARIANT`. Plus standing gates: the $10m/day tradability floor (S49's TRIAL-H5 lesson — re-measure the *control* at every liquidity corner, because the documented effect is strongest in exactly the small illiquid names that will not clear the floor) and an era split.

- **Strongest citation.** Andrea Frazzini (2006), "The Disposition Effect and Underreaction to News," *Journal of Finance* 61(4), 2017–2046 — builds the reference price from **mutual fund holdings**; post-event drift is most severe when capital gains and news share a sign; overhang spread ≈ **2.43%/month, t = 6.60**, negative overhang spread ≈ 0. Theory: Mark Grinblatt & Bing Han (2005), "Prospect Theory, Mental Accounting, and Momentum," *Journal of Financial Economics* 78(2), 311–339 (NBER WP 8734, 2002). *Honest caveat: both papers run on pre-2000 data, so "still alive after 2010" is the actual open question, not a given.*

## 2. Strategy crowding as an observable state variable (market ecology)

*(Complexity economics / Santa Fe — Farmer's "ecology of strategies".)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** A strategy's expected return is *density dependent* — it falls as the capital deployed in it rises — so the crowdedness of a factor, measured from who actually holds what, should predict that factor's next-period return better than the factor's own past return does.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** This is the most direct reframing available of Aegis's own recurring finding (b), "every edge was one era." Market ecology says the era is **not exogenous**: it is the wealth state of the ecosystem, and it is measurable. Scholl, Calinescu & Farmer compute the community matrix and find every diagonal entry negative (self-crowding) with magnitudes an order of magnitude apart — **−0.89 for noise traders vs −19.3 for trend followers** — i.e. trend following decays far faster under crowding than anything else, which is precisely the shape Aegis keeps hitting with momentum. Structurally, the predictor is a state of the *competitor population*, not a property of the price series, so it is not mechanically collinear with beta or with the factor itself. **The honest caveat is one the PNAS paper states about itself:** *"such a study requires counterparty identifiers on transactions in order to know who traded with whom... Trying to study a market ecology without such data is like trying to study a biological ecology in which one can observe that an animal ate another animal without any information about the types of animals involved."* Aegis has no counterparty identifiers — it has a quarterly, lagged, long-only-biased proxy. That is a real downgrade, so the test below is scoped to what the proxy supports: a **timing/decay** claim, not a community matrix.

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** The **13F institutional holdings panel** is the abundance data. Each quarter, score every 13F filer's portfolio on each of Aegis's existing factor definitions (momentum, value, the revision blend) by regressing its holding weights on factor z-scores; the cross-sectional sum of |loading| × AUM is that factor's **deployed wealth** — the species abundance. Then one panel regression over CRSP 1999–2024: `factor_return[t+1] ~ abundance[t] + Δabundance[t] + factor_return[t]`. Ecology predicts a **negative** coefficient on abundance and — the falsifiable part — a *larger magnitude for momentum/trend than for value*. Then re-run Aegis's own era-decay results with `abundance[t]` on the right-hand side and ask whether the era dummies stop mattering.

- **The control.** (1) **Shuffled-abundance** — re-run with each factor's abundance replaced by another factor's abundance, and by a phase-randomised surrogate of its own. If any abundance series predicts any factor's decay, it is a common AUM trend, not strategy-specific crowding. (2) **Total-AUM placebo** — replace abundance with total 13F AUM; if that alone explains the decay, the result is the growth of institutional assets, i.e. the era with a new name. (3) **Reverse causality** — abundance rises *because* the factor did well, so the negative coefficient could be pure flow mean-reversion; instrument abundance with its non-performance-driven component (flows into filers whose *other* sleeves did well) and require the sign to survive. (4) `n_effective` in date blocks: quarterly 13F over 26 years is ~104 observations per factor, and §58 binds hard. **This idea is at serious risk of failing its own power check — run §64 BEFORE confirmation, not after.**

- **Strongest citation.** Maarten P. Scholl, Anisoara Calinescu & J. Doyne Farmer (2021), "How market ecology explains market malfunction," *PNAS* 118(26), e2015574118 — community matrix, density-dependent returns, crowding as a negative diagonal. Foundational: J. Doyne Farmer (2002), "Market force, ecology and evolution," *Industrial and Corporate Change* 11(5), 895–953 — generalized Lotka–Volterra dynamics for capital flows between strategies, with the timescale for efficiency estimated at "years to decades."

## 3. The constraint owner: who captures margin when a supply chain binds

*(Operations research — Theory of Constraints / Goldratt, bottleneck and queueing views of supply chains.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** When an input becomes the binding constraint on an industry's throughput, the rent does not accrue to whoever is closest to the shortage — it accrues to the firm with the bargaining power to be served first, which empirically is the largest firm in the industry, so a shortage signal should be traded as **long the constraint-privileged incumbent, short its same-industry smaller competitors**.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** Three structural differences. First, it is an **explicitly relative, within-industry** trade — long superstar / short same-industry small caps, which nets out the industry factor and most of the market by construction, so killer (a) *beta in disguise* is addressed in the trade's design rather than in the post-hoc regression. Second, the trigger is an **exogenous physical event** (delivery delays, order backlogs) rather than a price or accounting variable, so it is not another rearrangement of the same price series Aegis has already exhausted. Third — and this is the genuinely OR-flavoured part — Goldratt's Theory of Constraints says the system's throughput is set by the single binding constraint and that *subordinating everything else to it* is where value is created; the finance translation is that during a shortage, suppliers ration by customer importance, which is a queueing-discipline fact, not a sentiment fact. Franzoni, Giannetti & Tubaldi document exactly this: suppliers prioritise large customers, superstars see *smaller* cost increases, higher markups, higher market share and **higher stock returns**, with a one-standard-deviation rise in delivery times worth about **35 bps/month** of superstar outperformance in the COVID window. **Honest caveat, and it is a serious one:** the effect's headline estimates lean heavily on 2021–22, which is precisely killer (b) — one era, and a spectacular one. The whole value of the test below is whether anything survives outside COVID. Also note this is *not* the Cohen–Frazzini customer-momentum mechanism that Aegis's S38d already closed: that lane asks whether a customer's *return* propagates to its supplier; this asks who captures *margin* at the binding constraint. Closing one does not close the other.

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** Aegis does not need a shortage database. Build the shortage indicator from the **~408k news headlines** plus the LLM stack it already runs locally at $0.00: classify each headline into {shortage / backlog / delivery delay / allocation / lead time} at the industry level, giving a monthly industry-level constraint-intensity series. Define "superstar" from **CRSP** market cap (top firm or top decile within SIC/GICS industry). Then form, each month, a within-industry long-short: long the top-size tercile, short the bottom two, only in industries whose constraint intensity is in the top quintile that month, holding one to three months. Returns from **CRSP** through 2024 and **Alpaca bars** 2025–26. As a cross-check on the mechanism rather than the trade, use **IBES** to verify that superstar margin/EPS revisions rise relative to their industry peers in constrained industries — if the fundamental leg does not move, the return leg is a coincidence.

- **The control.** (1) **The one that matters most: drop 2020-06 to 2022-12 entirely** and re-run. Per Aegis's own `SAME SIGN IN EVERY ERA IS NOT ALIVE TODAY` and the S49 era rules, report the effect by era with a decay t, not a pooled t. If the effect is COVID, the verdict is `RETIRED_FROM_CURRENT_SEARCH`, not a book. (2) **Size placebo:** run the identical within-industry size long-short in the *unconstrained* industries each month. Big-minus-small within industry is a known factor exposure; the claim is the *difference in differences*, constrained minus unconstrained, and that difference is the only number that should be quoted. (3) **Shuffled-industry control:** reassign each month's constraint intensity to a random industry and re-run — this catches the case where the LLM classifier is really detecting a macro regime that is common to all industries. (4) **Headline-count placebo:** replace the shortage classification with raw headline *volume* per industry; if attention alone reproduces the spread, the constraint content is doing no work.

- **Strongest citation.** Francesco Franzoni, Mariassunta Giannetti & Roberto Tubaldi (2023), "Supply Chain Shortages, Large Firms' Market Power, and Inflation," Swiss Finance Institute Research Paper No. 23-105 / CEPR Discussion Paper 18574 (SSRN 4586302) — suppliers prioritise large customers; superstars gain market share, markups, profitability and **stock returns** during shortages; ~35 bps/month superstar outperformance per 1 s.d. of delivery time in the COVID sample; the mechanism explains roughly a quarter of cross-industry inflation dispersion. Theoretical ancestor: Eliyahu M. Goldratt & Jeff Cox, *The Goal* (1984), and Goldratt's *Theory of Constraints* (1990) — throughput is set by the binding constraint. *(Note that Goldratt is a management text, not an empirical asset-pricing result; it supplies the framing, Franzoni et al. supply the evidence.)*

## 4. Size the book by the drawdown budget, not by the forecast: drawdown-constrained growth-optimal control

*(Control theory — Kelly and its variants; this one is not an alpha, it is a sizing law, and Aegis's own history says it needs one.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** Under a hard constraint that wealth never fall below a fraction α of its running maximum, the growth-optimal policy is not fractional Kelly at a fixed fraction but a **state-dependent** one — invest a constant proportion of the *surplus* `W − αM` above the drawdown floor, which mechanically de-risks into drawdowns and, notably, also reduces risk at all-time highs.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** It is not a return prediction at all, so killers (a)–(c) do not apply: the result is a theorem, not an estimate, and it requires no signal to be true. It is included because Aegis's *recorded* failures are disproportionately **sizing** failures, not alpha failures — the 28 Aug incident (twelve names × 25% = 300% gross, a 3% stop costing −9%, and the "fix" that widened the stop to a −24% worst case), the `A STOP INSIDE THE NOISE MAKES THE MIN HOLD UNREACHABLE` finding (hack6's 3% stop was 0.98 daily sd, 56.3% stopped out before session 10 of a 21-session thesis), and `A BUDGET THE BENCHMARK CANNOT MEET IS A BROKEN GATE` (G3's flat 0.35 drawdown refusal killed 188 of 207 genomes because the VW market itself drew down 47.2%). All three are the same defect: a **fixed** risk limit applied to a **path-dependent** quantity. Grossman–Zhou gives the correct object — a floor that moves with the running maximum and an exposure that is linear in the surplus above it — and Cherny & Obłój prove the constraint is *equivalent to a change in risk aversion*, i.e. Aegis's four declared personalities (preservation / balanced / aggressive / extreme growth) can be re-expressed as four values of α on one engine rather than four hand-tuned parameter sets. That directly serves the "one brain, several personalities" invariant. **Honest framing: this improves terminal wealth per unit of drawdown; it cannot manufacture an edge that is not there, and if applied to a zero-edge book it will lose money more slowly, which is not a result.**

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** No new data at all — it is a replay. Take the **overnight evolutionary/genetic search** logs (the `night_factory` genome banks) and the **six Alpaca paper books**, and re-run each surviving genome's realised return stream through three sizing rules: flat notional (status quo), fixed fractional Kelly, and the Azéma–Yor / Grossman–Zhou surplus rule at α ∈ {0.80, 0.65, 0.50}. Compare terminal wealth *at equal realised maximum drawdown* — which is Aegis's declared product ruler (the "two rulers" note: terminal wealth at a drawdown budget for the product). Then re-run the **G3 drawdown gate** with the surplus rule in place and count how many of the 188 refused genomes become admissible: if the answer is "most of them," the gate was the bug and this is worth shipping regardless of the sizing result.

- **The control.** (1) **Trailing-volatility sizing**, which Aegis already has and which S38g found *beat* the NN sizer while both lost to flat 1x — that is the incumbent to beat, not flat notional. If the surplus rule does not beat trailing-vol at equal drawdown, it is not earned. (2) **Randomised-return control:** feed the sizing rules i.i.d. bootstrapped versions of the same return streams (same mean, same variance, path destroyed). The surplus rule's advantage should *shrink* on path-destroyed data, because its whole claim is path dependence; if it performs identically, the gain was leverage, not control. (3) **Zero-edge control:** run all rules on a genome with edge set to exactly zero plus realistic costs. Every rule must lose; if the surplus rule "wins," the harness has a look-ahead in the running maximum (a real and easy bug — `M_t` must be the max *through t*, never the full-sample max). (4) Print the worst case in dollars for the largest admissible book before any of it touches a live lane, per the session-start protocol.

- **Strongest citation.** Sanford J. Grossman & Zhongquan Zhou (1993), "Optimal Investment Strategies for Controlling Drawdowns," *Mathematical Finance* 3(3), 241–276 — the optimal policy invests in the risky asset in proportion to the surplus `W_t − αM_t`. Generalisations: Jakša Cvitanić & Ioannis Karatzas (1995), "On portfolio optimization under drawdown constraints," *IMA Lecture Notes in Mathematics & Applications* 65, 35–46; Vladimir Cherny & Jan Obłój (2013), "Portfolio optimisation under non-linear drawdown constraints in a semimartingale financial model," *Finance and Stochastics* 17(4), 771–800 — the drawdown constraint is exactly equivalent to an unconstrained problem with a modified utility, giving the personalities-as-one-engine result. Origin: J. L. Kelly Jr. (1956), "A New Interpretation of Information Rate," *Bell System Technical Journal* 35(4), 917–926.

## 5. Mutual information as the SCREENING statistic for typed news events (and why it is a screen, not an edge)

*(Information theory — the Kelly–Thorp connection, MI instead of IC.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** Replace the information coefficient — a linear rank correlation that is blind to any non-monotone or conditional relationship — with the **mutual information `I(event_type; forward_return_bucket)`** as the statistic that decides which of Aegis's typed news-event categories are worth building a book on, because Kelly's theorem says the achievable increment in log-wealth growth from side information is *bounded above by exactly that mutual information*.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** The claim here is deliberately modest and it is about **search efficiency**, not about a new edge. IC is a linear statistic; a category whose informative signature is "big moves in either direction" or "matters only when the holder base is X" has an IC of approximately zero and will never be selected, which is one plausible reason a factor library scored by IC converged on 12-1 momentum for 99.5% of names (Aegis's own diagnosed bottleneck). MI is model-free, captures non-monotone and conditional structure, and — this is the part that makes it *disciplined* rather than just more flexible — it comes with a hard theoretical ceiling, so a category's MI translates into a **maximum plausible annualised growth contribution before any strategy is written**. That turns "is this worth building?" into an arithmetic question that can be answered in an afternoon, which is exactly the `P(changes the roadmap) × value − cost` framing in CLAUDE.md rule 5. **The honest limits, stated plainly.** (i) Barron & Cover prove `Δ ≤ I(X;Y)` and also give an explicit example with **I = 1 bit and Δ = 0** — a full bit of genuine information worth exactly nothing, because the informed asset is dominated. So MI is an upper bound that can be arbitrarily loose; a high-MI category is a *candidate*, never a finding. (ii) MI estimators are biased upward in finite samples and the bias grows with the number of bins, so a naive estimate over 408k headlines binned finely will manufacture "information" out of nothing. (iii) MI is gross of costs by construction. None of this is fatal for a screen; all of it is fatal for a claim.

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** This is the cheapest item in the whole document and it is a *meta*-test — it rescores work Aegis has already done. Take the **joined text-and-return panel built by E1** (339,657 cells, 3,031 names, 2025-01 to 2026-09 — the panel the programme spent months not having), type each cell's headline into the existing event taxonomy with the local Qwen model at $0.00, discretise forward 1-month returns into, say, five bins, and compute `I(type; return_bin)` per category with a **bias-corrected estimator (Miller–Madow or a k-NN/KSG estimator)**. Rank categories by MI and convert each to its wealth ceiling in %/yr via Kelly. Then do the one thing that makes it a real test rather than a ranking exercise: check whether the MI ranking would have **predicted the outcomes Aegis already knows** — did the categories behind the closed reaction lane, the closed revision lane and the surviving R2 monthly-digest lane sort correctly? If MI could not have called the corpses, it is not a better screen and the idea dies cheaply and honestly.

- **The control.** (1) **Shuffled-label null, and it is mandatory, not optional:** recompute MI with forward returns permuted *within date blocks* (preserving cross-sectional structure and the market factor), 1,000 times, and report each category's MI as a z-score against that null. Raw MI is uninterpretable without it because of the finite-sample bias above. (2) **Bin-count sweep:** report MI at 3, 5 and 10 bins; a category whose rank depends on bin count is an estimator artefact. (3) **IC head-to-head:** compute IC on the same panel. The idea only earns its keep if MI finds categories IC missed *and those categories then survive an actual book test* — MI beating IC on its own statistic is circular. (4) **Retrospective calibration**, as above: if the MI ranking does not separate Aegis's known survivors from its known corpses, stop.

- **Strongest citation.** Andrew R. Barron & Thomas M. Cover (1988), "A bound on the financial value of information," *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 34(5), 1097–1100 — proves `0 ≤ Δ ≤ I(X;Y)`, "each bit of information at most doubles the resulting wealth," and supplies the I = 1, Δ = 0 counterexample that keeps this honest. Origin: J. L. Kelly Jr. (1956), *Bell System Technical Journal* 35(4), 917–926 — max growth rate equals the rate of transmission of information. Practitioner bridge: Edward O. Thorp (2006), "The Kelly Criterion in Blackjack, Sports Betting, and the Stock Market," in *Handbook of Asset and Liability Management* (Zenios & Ziemba, eds.), North-Holland.

## 6. Narrative breadth sets the SHAPE of the drift, not its sign

*(Ecology/epidemiology — Shiller's narrative economics and SIR/SIS diffusion fitted to news-cascade data.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** If a story spreads through investors by word-of-mouth contagion, the serial correlation of returns is proportional to the *diffusion rate* of the story, so the **term structure of post-event drift is hump-shaped when the news started narrow** (few initial carriers, contagion accelerating) and **monotonically declining when the news started broad** — and news breadth at t=0 is observable before any of the drift happens.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** Every momentum and PEAD test Aegis has run asks a *level* question — is the drift positive? Hong, Hong & Ungureanu's prediction is about **shape**, and shape is far harder to mimic with beta or with a factor loading. Specifically: (a) *beta in disguise* — a market or size loading produces a monotone decay in autocorrelation, not a hump with a *medium-term peak*; the arm here is long narrow-news winners and short broad-news winners *at the medium horizon*, which is a difference-in-differences and cancels the common drift; (b) *one era* — contagion parameters can drift with the information environment (Twitter, Reddit, LLM-generated coverage), so this must be measured per era, but the *sign of the shape difference* is a structural prediction rather than a fitted level and is therefore a more demanding, more informative test; (c) *leakage* — news breadth is measured strictly at the event date from the headline corpus, with no forward information; (d) *tradability* — a medium-horizon monthly signal, which is the regime where Aegis's $10m floor is survivable. **The direct relevance to Aegis's one living lane:** R2 (a local 7B reading an anonymised *monthly* news digest, +16.19%/yr t 3.92 on 112 blocks) is currently a black box — nobody knows *why* a monthly digest works. If the mechanism is contagion, then R2's edge should be concentrated in names whose digest content was **narrowly** covered, and should be near zero for wall-to-wall stories. That is a testable explanation for the only thing currently alive, which is worth more than a new lane. **Honest caveat on the wider field:** Shiller's 2017 AEA address is a research programme, not a result, and he says so ("difficult to study in a scientific manner"). The nearest careful empirical test — Das & Kim's NBER WP 30195, which builds crash-narrative prevalence from Doc2Vec on the WSJ with explicit look-ahead controls — finds crash narratives predict **investor attention and future VIX** but reports **no significant relationship to future market returns**. Narrative economics currently has better evidence for predicting *volatility and attention* than *returns*. Aegis should expect a vol/dispersion result, not a directional one, and should say so in the pre-registration.

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** Define breadth at the event date from the **~408k news headline corpus**: for each (name, month), `breadth = distinct outlets covering it / its trailing median`, or simply the headline count percentile. Split events into narrow (bottom tercile) and broad (top tercile). Then estimate the **term structure of non-overlapping monthly autocorrelation** — the drift at months 1, 2, 3, 6, 12 after the event — separately in each breadth bucket, on **CRSP** returns through 2024 and **Alpaca bars** 2025–26, restricted to the E1 joined text-and-return panel where both exist. The prediction, stated in advance: *narrow* → peak at the medium horizon (roughly months 2–4); *broad* → monotone decline from month 1. Add turnover from CRSP as the third leg, since HHU's third prediction is that the hump is most prominent conditional on **low turnover**. Then, separately and cheaply: re-slice **R2's existing 112 blocks** by digest breadth and report the edge in each half.

- **The control.** (1) **Shuffled-breadth control:** permute the breadth label across names *within each month*, preserving the month's return distribution entirely. The hump must vanish. This is the single most important control because breadth correlates with size and liquidity, and a size-sorted autocorrelation term structure will differ for reasons that have nothing to do with contagion. (2) **Size/liquidity-matched control:** re-run with narrow and broad buckets matched on market cap and dollar volume — if the shape difference dies on matching, the mechanism is illiquidity, which Aegis has already priced. (3) **Public-vs-private decomposition**, which is HHU's own design: split the event return into an earnings-explained component (IBES) and a residual. The theory says the *public* component's drift declines monotonically and the *residual* component's drift humps. Getting the same shape in both legs falsifies the mechanism even if both legs pay. (4) Because this is a shape claim over five horizons per bucket, it is a multiple-comparison problem: screen with BH-FDR, export with Holm (§63), and count `n_effective` in date blocks (§58).

- **Strongest citation.** Dong Hong, Harrison Hong & Andrei Ungureanu (2011, AFA 2012 Chicago Meetings Paper; SSRN 1569418) — "An Epidemiological Approach to Opinion and Price-Volume Dynamics": serial correlation of returns is proportional to the word-of-mouth diffusion rate; hump-shaped term structure for narrowly-released news, monotone decline for widely-released news; tested and confirmed on US stock data. Framing: Robert J. Shiller (2017), "Narrative Economics," *American Economic Review* 107(4), 967–1004 — imports Kermack & McKendrick's (1927) SIR compartmental model to economic belief contagion. Negative/limiting evidence to pre-register against: Das & Kim (NBER Working Paper 30195, 2022, rev. Nov 2024) — crash narratives predict attention and VIX, **not returns**. Method for fitting cascades: Rizoiu et al. (2018), "SIR-Hawkes: Linking Epidemic Models and Hawkes Processes for Information Diffusion in a Finite Population," *WWW '18* — the SIR infection rate equals a finite-population Hawkes intensity once recovery events are marginalised out, which is what lets you fit a contagion model to headline arrivals alone.

## 7. Square-root impact: not an edge, but the cost model Aegis is currently missing

*(Statistical mechanics of order flow — Bouchaud, Farmer, Lillo. Included with an explicit "this is not alpha" label.)*

- **Mechanism in one sentence.** The price impact of a metaorder of size Q grows as **√Q**, not linearly, and is largely independent of execution time — so every backtest that charges a flat per-trade or per-day cost is wrong in a direction that depends on position size, and is wrong by a *lot* at both ends.

- **Why it might survive where momentum/value did not.** **It is not a return-predicting mechanism and should not be pitched as one.** State that plainly: the square-root law is a *cost* law, and Bouchaud's own reading of it (the "Order-Driven" theory of excess volatility) is that most impact is mechanical rather than informational, which if anything argues *against* reading order flow as a signal. What earns its place here is that Aegis has a documented, expensive, still-open cost-model defect: S51 found **C2's net −237%/yr was an artefact of a flat 100 bps/day cost**, and the 2026-09-07 night lab found that moving turnover from 0.13 to 0.49 bought +4.67pp/yr on revisions while **85 of 100 broad books lost** — both of those are cost-model-shaped results, and both would change under a concave impact model. The square-root law is also as close to a settled empirical regularity as microstructure has: Bucci, Benzaquen, Lillo & Bouchaud establish the linear→square-root crossover on **8 million institutional metaorders** in US equities, and the law is documented to be "remarkably independent of asset class, time period, execution style and market venue," including through the arrival of HFT. **Honest caveat, and it is why this is idea 7 and not idea 1:** the *other* half of the stat-mech-of-order-flow programme — reading endogeneity/reflexivity out of a Hawkes branching ratio as an early-warning signal — is in open dispute between two competent groups. Hardiman, Bercot & Bouchaud (2013) find the branching ratio pinned at n ≈ 1 and **constant** from 1998–2011, contradicting Filimonov & Sornette (2012), who find it rising from ~0.3 to ~0.7–0.8; Filimonov & Sornette (2013) reply that power-law kernels are upward-biased by outliers and edge effects and that *a mixture of pure Poisson processes with regime shifts calibrates to n ≈ 1 when the true n is exactly 0*. Both sides demonstrate the other's estimator is biased. **Aegis should treat the branching-ratio-as-signal lane as unresolved and stay out of it**; the square-root impact law is the part of this literature that is not in dispute.

- **Cheapest test on Aegis's actual data.** Aegis has **TAQ through 2024**, which is what this needs, and it does not need a metaorder database to get the useful 80%. Step one, calibration: for each name-day, compute realised daily volatility σ and daily volume V from CRSP/TAQ, and fit the standard form `impact = Y·σ·(Q/V)^δ` by binning Aegis's own paper-book fills from the **six Alpaca books** by participation rate Q/V and regressing realised slippage; check whether δ̂ lands near 0.5 and whether Y is order unity, as the literature says it should be. Step two, and this is the part that changes decisions: **re-run the portfolio farm and the G3 genome bank under the concave cost model instead of the flat one** and recount winners. The specific question with a roadmap-changing answer is whether the 85-of-100 losing broad books and the C2 −237%/yr result survive; if they were flat-cost artefacts, a large block of already-paid-for computation becomes re-interpretable at near-zero marginal cost. Step three: replace the fixed `TRADABLE_DOLLAR_VOL` $3m/$10m floor with a **capacity curve** — for each candidate book, the notional at which √-impact eats the edge — which is a strictly more informative gate than a binary floor.

- **The control.** (1) **Linear-impact control:** re-run everything under Kyle-linear impact calibrated to the same average cost. If the ranking of books is unchanged between linear and square-root, the cost model was never the binding constraint and this is not worth shipping. (2) **Flat-cost control:** keep the status quo flat cost as the third arm, so all three appear side by side in the receipt. (3) **Out-of-fit validation:** calibrate Y and δ on 1999–2015 fills and evaluate slippage prediction on 2016–2024 — a cost model fitted and evaluated on the same trades is the cost-side version of the mistake in CANON. (4) **Guard against the seductive error:** a concave cost model makes *every* strategy look better, so any book that flips from loser to winner under the new costs must then clear the *same* significance and control bars it would have had to clear originally — it does not inherit a pass. Per CLAUDE.md, `portfolio_farm.Policy` must still refuse zero costs, and the impact parameters must travel on every result row exactly as `zero_cost_diagnostic` does.

- **Strongest citation.** Frédéric Bucci, Michael Benzaquen, Fabrizio Lillo & Jean-Philippe Bouchaud (2019), "Crossover from Linear to Square-Root Market Impact," *Physical Review Letters* 122(10), 108302 — 8 million institutional metaorders, clean linear→√ crossover matching the locally-linear-order-book theory. Theory: Bence Tóth, Yves Lempérière, Cyril Deremble, Joachim de Lataillade, Julien Kockelkoren & Jean-Philippe Bouchaud (2011), "Anomalous Price Impact and the Critical Nature of Liquidity in Financial Markets," *Physical Review X* 1(2), 021006. For the unresolved reflexivity dispute, cite **both** sides: Vladimir Filimonov & Didier Sornette (2012), *Physical Review E* 85, 056108, versus Stephen J. Hardiman, Nicolas Bercot & Jean-Philippe Bouchaud (2013), "Critical reflexivity in financial markets: a Hawkes process analysis," *European Physical Journal B* 86, 442 — and the rebuttal, Filimonov & Sornette (2015), "Apparent criticality and calibration issues in the Hawkes self-excited point process model," *Quantitative Finance* 15(8), 1293–1314 (arXiv 1308.6756).
