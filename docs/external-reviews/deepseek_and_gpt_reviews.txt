# PART 1 — VALIDATION: ATTACK THE RESULTS

## 1.1 The multiple-testing arithmetic: mostly right, with one critical nuance the dossier itself caught but buried

The dossier's Z(179) = 2.729 derivation from Bailey & López de Prado (2014) is **mathematically correct** under the null of no skill. The Monte Carlo cross-check (200k draws → 2.7097 vs 2.7291) is credible. The conclusion — "on a 15-year explore window, 179 pure-noise strategies produce a best-of-set annualised Sharpe of ≈0.71 in expectation" — follows.

**However, the dossier's own F1 correction reveals a deeper problem:** the multiple-testing arithmetic is being applied to the *wrong population*. The 179 candidates are not 179 independent draws from the same null distribution. They are **clustered by family** (insider, options, momentum, 13D, etc.), and within each family, candidates share data sources, construction logic, and economic priors.

| Issue | Dossier's treatment | Correct treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Independent trials assumption | Counts 179 as 179 | Effective N after clustering is much lower |
| Correlation across candidates | Ignored | Bailey-LdP's σ_SR should be **empirical**, not null |
| Family-level search | Not counted separately | Each family is a separate multiple-testing problem |

The dossier acknowledges this in V-C.1: *"the argument runs against you: 179 is a floor, because it counts registered candidates, not the parameter variations, lookback windows, and universe choices tried within each."* **This is correct and should be the paper's headline.**

**The actionable correction:** compute the empirical standard deviation of the 179 candidate Sharpes and use it in the DSR formula. The dossier flags this as a one-line job. **Do it. It will make the DSRs even lower.**

## 1.5 The 37-day paper-lane record: the dossier's framing is correct

**This is mathematically correct.** SE(annualised Sharpe) ≈ 1/√(T/252) = 1/√(55/252) = 2.14.

**Minimum forward window before ANY claim is meaningful:**

| Confidence | Sharpe needed for t=2 | Trading days needed |
|---|---|---|
| 95% | 2.00 | **252 (1 year)** |
| 95% | 1.00 | **1,008 (4 years)** |
| 95% | 0.50 | **4,032 (16 years)** |

**The 24-month no-skill-claims rule is not conservatism. It is the arithmetic minimum.** At 24 months, SE ≈ 1/√(504/252) = 0.71, so a Sharpe of 1.42 is needed for t=2. **The lanes cannot distinguish skill from noise for at least 2–4 years.**

## 2.3 Is anything over-disciplined?

**Yes: the random-date placebo gate may have a false-kill rate that's too high.**

The dossier's own F1 correction shows the five placebo seeds *agree with each other* — the mean effect is consistent across random draws. This suggests the gate is detecting **cohort drag** (a real economic phenomenon) rather than **timing information** (the thing it's supposed to isolate).

**The problem:** a placebo that shuffles dates destroys *both* timing information *and* the calendar-month marginal of the real events. The uniform redraw gives the placebo a different calendar composition from the real arm. **So the gate compares the real arm to a cohort that is not calendar-matched, and then attributes the difference to "timing" when it could be cohort composition.**

**The fix:** replace with a **permutation placebo** (shuffle real event dates *across* permnos, preserving the calendar marginal exactly). This is what the dossier recommends in F2. **Do it.**

## 4.3 Extend: ideas you haven't had

### Idea 1: BoardEx network signal (highest value, data in hand)

**Hypothesis:** Firms whose boards are well-connected (via shared education, employment, or board interlocks) outperform, and the effect is concentrated around corporate news announcements.

**Construction:**
1. Build the network graph as-of each rebalance date using `boardex_company_networks` with `"Curr"` sentinel handling (a look-ahead trap).
2. Compute each firm's **network centrality** (eigenvector centrality, degree centrality) within the graph.
3. Form long-short portfolios on centrality.

## 4.4 Regime switching: is there a version that works?

**Yes — regime-conditional sizing, not regime switching.**

The evidence (Shu-Yu-Mulvey 2024) shows that regime switching **reduces risk** (volatility and max drawdown) but does **not enhance returns**. The HMM-guided strategy lost 1.7pp/yr to buy-and-hold.

**The version that works:**
1. **Use the regime signal for sizing only.** Reduce equity exposure when the regime is estimated to be "bad"; increase it when "good."
2. **Never switch between strategies.** Switch between *exposure levels* of the same strategy.
3. **Use filtered probabilities, not smoothed probabilities.** Smoothed probabilities use future data (a look-ahead).
4. **Add a switching penalty** to prevent excessive turnover. The jump model (JM) does this — the dossier's §15 and §18 tested it and rejected it, but the JM's risk-reduction properties are still valid.
5. **Pre-register the switching rule and the penalty parameter.** The JM's optimal jump penalty is a tuned hyperparameter — tune it by time-series CV only and haircut by √(2 ln N).

**Expected effect:** Sharpe improvement of ~0.08-0.11 (Harvey et al. 2018). Not 0.5. **The bigger benefit is left-tail truncation.**

## 4.5 Risk appetite: rigorous treatment

The owner asks: *"Can we make very risky moves and just maximize ROI?"*

**The rigorous answer:**

### The objective: geometric growth

$$g = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$$

This is the correct objective for a compounding investor. Sharpe is a portfolio-selection statistic that assumes you can lever freely. If you cannot lever (or you are compounding a single book), the geometric growth rate is what matters.

### The Kelly criterion

For US equities ($\mu_{excess} = 6\%$, $\sigma = 16\%$):
$$f^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.06}{0.0256} = 2.34\times$$

Growth at fraction $c$ of Kelly:
$$g(c) = (2c - c^2) \cdot g^*$$

| $c$ | Leverage | % of max growth | Portfolio vol |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 1.17× | 75.0% | 18.8% |
| 1.00 | 2.34× | 100% | 37.5% |
| 2.00 | 4.69× | 0% | 75.0% |

**The curve is flat on the left and catastrophic on the right.** Half-Kelly keeps 75% of growth at half the vol. Double-Kelly has zero long-run growth.

### The estimation-error penalty

Even with 50 years of data, the 1-sd band on your Kelly estimate spans 1.46× to 3.23×. Since overbetting loss is quadratic and underbetting loss is nearly flat, the asymmetry alone forces you well below the point estimate.

**Half-Kelly is the defensible upper bound.** Quarter-Kelly if $\mu$ comes from a backtest.

### The human-capital adjustment (Merton/Campbell-Viceira)

A 19-year-old with large human capital (future labour income) and a 30-year horizon **can** rationally take more risk than a retiree. The lifecycle literature (Merton 1969; Campbell & Viceira 2002; Ayres & Nalebuff 2010) shows that human capital is a bond-like asset, so the optimal allocation to equities is higher when human capital is large relative to financial wealth.

**But:** this justifies **1.0–1.3× exposure to a diversified book**. It does **not** justify 25% in one micro-cap.

### The line between "aggressive" and "innumerate"

| Action | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1.0× notional in a 50-stock book | **Aggressive but rational** |
| 1.2× notional in a 50-stock book | **Aggressive but rational** (half-Kelly) |
| 25% position in one micro-cap | **Innumerate** — idiosyncratic risk is uncompensated |
| 2.0× leverage on a 50-stock book | **Innumerate** — double-Kelly has zero long-run growth |
| Peak-detection ("exit at the top") | **Innumerate** — the project's own §1 refutes this |

**The mirror lane's −22.4% at beta 1.05 is the receipt:** a −24pp purely idiosyncratic loss. **That is what uncompensated risk looks like.**
